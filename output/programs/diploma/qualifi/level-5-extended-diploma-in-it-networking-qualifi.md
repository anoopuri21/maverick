## 2.2 `description`

```html
<p>Networks have a habit of being understood in pieces. Someone knows how to crimp a cable, someone else configures switches, a third person drew the topology years ago and has left. Studying the subject properly means seeing it as one thing, from the physical layer up to a design that somebody can hand over.</p>
<p>That arc is visible in the published subject list. Logical IT networking and physical IT networking establish how networks are reasoned about and how they are actually built. System administration covers keeping the machines on them running. Network routing and switching deals with moving traffic between them. Network design and administration asks you to plan the whole thing.</p>
<p>Seven further subjects surround that arc, and they are the same ones Qualifi puts beneath every IT award it runs at this level: the ethics and principles of the field, the maths and statistics behind it, hardware and operating systems, graphics work with database concepts, security, development in C#.NET, and a subject on building a technology business. Nothing is optional. Qualifi writes that all twelve are mandatory to receive 240 credits, under number 610/3475/7.</p>
```

## 4. `faqs` *(6)*

**Are all twelve subjects really compulsory?**
<p>Yes, and Qualifi says so in as many words on the qualification page: all twelve are mandatory to receive 240 credits. There is no optional group and no decision for you to make. If you would rather choose part of your programme, Qualifi's general information technology award at the same level offers two options at each stage instead.</p>

**How much of it is actually about networks?**
<p>Four of the twelve subjects are networking proper — logical, physical, routing and switching, and design and administration. System administration and network security sit close by. The remaining six are shared general IT: ethics, maths and statistics, PC maintenance, graphics and databases, programming, and the entrepreneurship subject. So expect roughly half the programme to be general rather than specialist, and plan accordingly if you were expecting a pure networking course.</p>

**Why does the overview mention elective choices?**
<p>Because Qualifi uses one overview paragraph across all four of its IT awards at this level, and that paragraph was written with the general version in mind. It does not describe this qualification, where nothing is optional. We are pointing it out rather than repeating it, because reading that sentence and expecting choice here would set you up for a surprise.</p>

**Does it cover Cisco or similar certifications?**
<p>No alignment with any vendor programme is published by Qualifi, so none should be assumed. The content plainly overlaps with what vendor certifications address — routing, switching, network design — but overlap is not the same as recognition or exemption. Vendor certifications run their own examinations on their own syllabuses. Many network engineers hold both kinds of credential; plan for them separately.</p>

**What is technopreneurship doing in a networking qualification?**
<p>It is part of the compulsory second-stage set shared across all of Qualifi's IT awards at this level, so it arrives with the family rather than being chosen for this specialism. The reasoning is not unreasonable: technical people who understand how a business is started and run make better decisions about cost and priority. If you want nothing but networks, know that this subject is in the programme.</p>

**Can I get in without qualifications?**
<p>Qualifi lists none as mandatory. Three things are published: an age floor of eighteen, an English standard of IELTS 6 or its equivalent for non-native speakers, and two supporting references, ideally with an academic among them. Your centre then forms its own view about whether you can cope with the material. Self-taught network engineers are exactly the kind of applicant this structure suits.</p>
